A plain-language walkthrough

How 10k-analyst Works

From raw SEC filings to a trustworthy, cited answer — explained the way you'd explain it to a friend, not a textbook.
Every year, public companies like Apple and Microsoft have to publish a giant legal document called a 10-K that lists, among other things, every risk that could hurt the business. These documents are hundreds of pages long and written in dense legal language. 10k-analyst is a program that reads those filings, understands them, and lets you ask plain-English questions about them — and it's built so that it never just makes up an answer. Every fact it gives you is backed by a real sentence from a real filing, checked by code before you ever see it.

The big picture

What problem is this actually solving?

Why this program exists, in one page

Imagine you wanted to know what Apple thinks could go wrong with its business this year. The honest answer is somewhere inside a 150-page legal document on a government website. Nobody has time to read that. So the easy shortcut is to paste the document into a chatbot and ask it to summarize. The problem is that chatbots are known to hallucinate — they sometimes make up facts, numbers, or quotes that sound completely real but aren't actually in the source. In a casual setting that's annoying. In finance, where a made-up number could change someone's decision, it's dangerous.

10k-analyst exists to fix that specific problem. It doesn't trust any single AI model to both find the right information and tell the truth about it. Instead, it splits the job into small pieces and gives each piece to whichever tool is actually good at it:

The core idea: fast yes/no and multiple-choice judgments (is this paragraph relevant? is this topic about cybersecurity?) go to a specialized decision model called Jev. Open-ended writing goes to a general chatbot (Claude). And anything involving a number — dollars, percentages, dates — is checked by plain, old-fashioned code, because no AI model should be trusted to do arithmetic correctly every single time.

That's the whole philosophy of the project in one sentence: use AI for judgment, not for facts, and let code double-check everything that can be double-checked. The rest of this document walks through how that idea turns into actual working software, phase by phase, and then explains why Jev specifically was picked for the judgment calls instead of just using one chatbot for everything.

The journey of a question

From a filing to an answer, step by step

What actually happens when you type a question into the app
1. Download. The program grabs real 10-K filings straight from the SEC's own website (EDGAR) for a list of companies.
↓
2. Cut out the useful parts. A 10-K has dozens of sections; only two actually matter here — "Risk Factors" and "Management's Discussion." Everything else is thrown away.
↓
3. Chop into paragraphs. Those two sections get split into individual paragraphs, each with its own ID tag, like a library call number.
↓
4. Label every paragraph. Jev reads each paragraph and tags it with a topic (is this about cybersecurity? supply chains? lawsuits?) and a tone score (how alarming does this sound?).
↓
5. Turn paragraphs into searchable math. Every paragraph is converted into a list of numbers (an "embedding") that captures its meaning, and stored in a database built for fast similarity search.
↓
6. You ask a question. The question gets turned into the same kind of number-list, and the database finds the paragraphs whose meaning is closest to it.
↓
7. Jev double-checks relevance. Just because a paragraph is mathematically "close" doesn't mean it's actually useful — Jev reviews each one and flags anything it's confident is off-topic.
↓
8. Claude writes the answer. The chatbot writes a real, readable answer — but it's required to cite exactly which paragraph IDs it used, like footnotes.
↓
9. Code checks every number. Any dollar amount, percentage, or figure in the answer gets pulled out and checked against the actual cited text. If a number isn't really there, it gets flagged.
↓
10. Jev gives a final grounding check. Jev reviews the finished answer one more time for whether it's actually supported by the sources. If confidence is too low, the app shows a warning instead of pretending everything is fine.

That's it — ten steps, each one small and easy to test on its own, that together turn a messy legal document into a trustworthy answer with receipts.

Scaffold

Built the tool that downloads real 10-K filings from the SEC and slices out just the Risk Factors and MD&A sections, splitting them into clean paragraphs. Ran against 6 real companies with zero manual copy-pasting.

Every paragraph got a risk-topic label and a tone score from Jev, plus a math-vector of its meaning, all saved to the database so paragraphs could later be filtered and searched.

The payoff

Why does any of this matter?

Who would actually want to use something like this

Reading 10-K filings is part of how investors, analysts, journalists, and even students doing research try to understand a company before trusting it with their money, their attention, or their recommendation to someone else. The problem has never been that the information doesn't exist — it's that it's buried in hundreds of pages of dense legal writing that almost nobody actually reads in full.

10k-analyst matters because it closes the gap between "the answer technically exists in a public document" and "a normal person can actually get that answer in ten seconds, and trust it." A few concrete groups who'd get real value out of a tool like this:

Everyday investors who want to understand a company's risks before buying stock, without paying for an expensive research terminal or reading 150 pages themselves.
Students and researchers learning how real companies describe risk, who need quick, citation-backed answers instead of wading through raw filings.
Analysts and journalists who need a fast first pass across many companies, but still need to know exactly which sentence backs up any claim before they'd ever repeat it publicly.

What makes this different from "just ask a chatbot" is the trust layer built around the answer. A regular chatbot will confidently answer even when it's wrong. This program is built to do the opposite: when it isn't sure, it says so, flags the specific problem, or refuses to answer at all rather than guessing. That's the entire point — an answer you can actually rely on is worth more than an answer that merely sounds confident.

Under the hood

What makes Jev different from a regular chatbot?

Same question, two very different kinds of AI

It helps to think of these two AI tools like two different employees with two very different jobs. A general chatbot (like ChatGPT or Claude) is like a skilled writer — you can ask it almost anything, and it'll give you a thoughtful, well-written response. But ask it "how sure are you?" and it's just guessing out loud, because it was never built to actually measure its own confidence. Jev is more like a very fast, very consistent referee — you give it a narrow question with a fixed set of possible answers ("is this paragraph about cybersecurity, yes or no?"), and it gives you back an answer plus a real, calibrated confidence number that's been measured to actually mean what it says.

The takeaway

Why Jev specifically was the right call here

Three reasons this project leans on Jev instead of just using one chatbot for everything

It can't lie by accident

Because Jev only ever picks from a fixed list of answers you give it, it structurally cannot wander off and invent a brand-new, made-up answer the way free-text writing can. That's not a promise — it's a built-in limit on what it's even capable of outputting.

Its confidence actually means something

When Jev says it's 90% sure, that number has been calibrated — meaning it's been checked to actually line up with how often it's right. A regular chatbot's confidence is more like a vibe; Jev's is closer to an actual measurement.

It's fast and cheap enough to use constantly

This project runs Jev on every single paragraph of every filing — thousands of small decisions. That only works because Jev is built to be fast and cheap per call. Doing that with a full chatbot call every time would be slow and expensive at this scale.

None of this means Jev is simply "better" than a chatbot in general — it's better at this specific kind of job: small, bounded, repeatable decisions where a trustworthy confidence number matters more than beautiful writing. The chatbot is still the one who writes the final answer you actually read. The real lesson of this whole project is less "which AI is best" and more "match the right tool to the right part of the job" — which is exactly what separates a program you can actually trust from one that just sounds convincing.
